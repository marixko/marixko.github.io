## Random number generation

Module 2, on computational methods, begins here. Almost every modern
statistical method (Monte Carlo, *bootstrap*, permutation tests, MCMC) needs to
generate samples from probability distributions. Everything rests on a single
primitive: a generator of uniform numbers on $$(0,1)$$.

### Contents

- Pseudorandom numbers
- Inverse transform
- Acceptance-rejection method
- Box-Muller transform
- Sampling vectors and discrete distributions

## Pseudorandom numbers

A computer is deterministic, so the "random numbers" it produces are actually a
**pseudorandom** sequence: generated by a deterministic recurrence from a
**seed**, but statistically indistinguishable from an i.i.d. sequence of
$$\text{Uniform}(0,1)$$. Modern generators (the Mersenne Twister, PCG) have an
astronomically long period and pass batteries of randomness tests. Fixing the
seed makes any simulation **reproducible**.

From here on, we assume a stream $$U_1,U_2,\ldots$$ of independent uniforms is
available, and the problem is to transform it into samples from the desired
distribution.

## Inverse transform

The basic method, already seen in lecture 1.6. If $$X$$ has CDF $$F$$ and we
want to sample from $$X$$, we use the quantile function $$F^{-1}$$:

$$
\boxed{
U\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)\ \Longrightarrow\ X=F^{-1}(U)\ \text{has CDF }F.
}
$$

Just one uniform per sample. Examples:

- **Exponential$$(\lambda)$$**: $$F(x)=1-e^{-\lambda x}$$, so
  $$X=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\log(1-U)$$ (or $$-\frac{1}{\lambda}\log U$$, since
  $$1-U$$ is also uniform);
- **Discrete**: $$X=x_k$$ when
  $$\sum_{j<k}p_j\le U<\sum_{j\le k}p_j$$.

The limitation is needing $$F^{-1}$$ in closed or numerical form; for the
normal, for example, $$F^{-1}$$ is not elementary.

## Acceptance-rejection method

Works for any density $$f$$ whose value we can evaluate, even without
$$F^{-1}$$. We choose a **proposal** density $$g$$, easy to sample, and a
constant $$M$$ such that $$f(x)\le M\,g(x)$$ for all $$x$$. The algorithm:

1. sample $$Y$$ from $$g$$ and $$U$$ from $$\text{Uniform}(0,1)$$;
2. if $$U\le\dfrac{f(Y)}{M\,g(Y)}$$, accept $$X=Y$$; otherwise, repeat.

The accepted samples have exactly the density $$f$$. The acceptance probability
is $$1/M$$, so the more tightly $$g$$ "envelopes" $$f$$, the more efficient the
method.

## Box-Muller transform

A trick specific to the normal. If $$U_1,U_2$$ are independent uniforms, then

$$
Z_1=\sqrt{-2\log U_1}\,\cos(2\pi U_2),
\qquad
Z_2=\sqrt{-2\log U_1}\,\sin(2\pi U_2)
$$

are two independent $$\mathcal{N}(0,1)$$. To obtain
$$\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})$$, just use $$\mu+\sigma Z$$.

## Vectors and derived cases

- **Multivariate normal** $$\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)$$: factor
  $$\Sigma=LL^{\top}$$ (Cholesky decomposition), sample
  $$z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$$ and return $$x=\mu+Lz$$.
- **Gamma, chi-square, $$t$$, $$F$$, Beta**: built from sums, ratios and
  squares of normals and exponentials, following the relations of lecture 1.9.
- **Sampling without replacement** and **random permutations**: the
  Fisher-Yates shuffle, the basis of the resampling methods of the next
  lectures.
